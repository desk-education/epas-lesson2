Introduction to EPAS

Electronic Products Assembly and Servicing (EPAS) requires precision, safety, and a thorough understanding of the tools involved. Using the correct tool not only ensures the quality and longevity of electronic repairs and assemblies but also protects the technician from injury and the components from damage.

Click on any highlighted tool name or image below to enlarge it in the lightbox viewer!

Explore the Equipment Categories

Hand tools are the foundational instruments for physical manipulation of electronic components, wires, and chassis hardware.

  • Pliers: Long-nose pliers are essential for holding tiny components, bending component leads, and retrieving dropped items in tight spaces. Side cutters (diagonal clippers) are used strictly for cutting wires and component leads flush to a circuit board.
  • Screwdrivers: Electronics require both standard (Phillips and flathead) screwdrivers for chassis screws and precision screwdrivers (sometimes called jeweler's screwdrivers) for the tiny screws holding internal components like hard drives or laptop casings.
  • Wire Strippers: Used to cleanly remove the plastic insulation from wires without nicking the conductive copper core beneath.

Standard Hand Tools

These tools are specifically designed to handle delicate electronic tasks safely without damaging micro-components.

  • Tweezers: Precision, anti-magnetic tweezers are necessary for handling surface-mount technology (SMT) components, placing small screws, and positioning wires.
  • Magnifying Lamp: A lighted magnifying glass, often mounted on an articulated arm, prevents eye strain and allows the technician to inspect tiny solder joints for micro-cracks or bridging.
  • Anti-Static Tools: Brushes designed not to generate a static charge are used to clean dust off PCBs (Printed Circuit Boards).

Precision electronics work

Accurate measurement is critical for both assembly and troubleshooting. Without these, fixing electronics is just guessing.

  • Multimeter (VOM): The most indispensable tool in EPAS. It measures Voltage (volts), Current (amps), and Resistance (ohms). Digital multimeters (DMMs) also feature continuity testing.
  • Oscilloscope: While a multimeter measures steady values, an oscilloscope visually graphs electrical signals over time. It is crucial for diagnosing complex issues where the timing or shape of a signal is erratic.
  • LCR Meter: Specifically measures Inductance (L), Capacitance (C), and Resistance (R). It is highly accurate for testing individual passive components.

Standard Digital Multimeter (DMM)

Knowing what a tool is called is only the first step; knowing how to use and maintain it prevents costly mistakes.

Identification

Technicians must be able to visually distinguish tools. Using the wrong tool (e.g., wire cutter vs bolt cutter) ruins both the tool and the workpiece.

Proper Use

A screwdriver is not a pry bar or a chisel. Pliers should not be used to tighten nuts, as they will strip the metal; a wrench or nut driver should be used instead.

Maintenance

Hand tools should be kept clean and dry to prevent rust. Moving joints (like those on pliers) require occasional a drop of light machine oil. Test leads on measuring instruments must be regularly checked for frayed insulation, which poses a severe shock hazard.

Soldering is the process of joining two metals together using a filler metal (solder) with a lower melting point.

  • Soldering Iron/Station: A soldering iron applies heat to the joint. A soldering station provides adjustable temperature control, crucial for modern, heat-sensitive components.
  • Solder and Flux: Solder is the alloy that forms the electrical and mechanical bond. Flux cleans the metal surfaces of oxidation during heating, allowing the solder to flow smoothly.
  • Desoldering Tools: When components must be removed, a desoldering pump (solder sucker) uses a vacuum to pull up molten solder, or copper desoldering wick uses capillary action to absorb it.

Soldering Safety:

  1. Ventilation: Solder flux creates fumes that can cause respiratory issues. Always solder in a well-ventilated area or use a fume extractor fan.
  2. Heat Management: A soldering iron tip operates between 300°C and 400°C. Never leave it unattended, always return it to its stand.
  3. Eye Protection: Wear safety glasses. Molten solder and hot flux can occasionally spit or splatter.

Applying solder to PCB joints

A disorganized workstation is a hazard to both the technician and the electronics. An effective EPAS workstation often relies on the 5S Methodology: Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardize, and Sustain.

  • ESD Safety: Electrostatic Discharge (ESD) is the hidden killer of electronics. Walking across a carpet can generate thousands of volts of static electricity—harmless to humans, but instantly destructive to a microchip. A proper workstation requires an anti-static mat on the desk and an anti-static wrist strap worn by the technician, both properly grounded.
  • Ergonomics and Lighting: The workstation should be at a comfortable height to prevent back strain during long repair sessions. Shadow-free, bright lighting is mandatory for intricate detail work.
  • Tool Shadowing: Tools should be returned to designated spots, often outlined on a pegboard or in foam drawer inserts, so a missing tool is immediately obvious.

How to Use a Digital Multimeter

Testing components with a digital multimeter (DMM) requires understanding three core measurements: voltage, resistance, and continuity. Doing this correctly prevents damage to the meter and the circuit you are working on.

Click 'Start Procedure' below to begin the step-by-step interactive guides.

Voltage (DC/AC)

Voltage testing checks how much electrical pressure is present. For electronics (like a battery or circuit board), use DC Voltage (V with a solid/dashed line). For wall outlets, use AC Voltage (V with a wavy line).

Step 1 of 4

Plug in the probes

Insert the black probe into the common (COM) port. Insert the red probe into the port marked with a V.

Step 2 of 4

Set the dial to Voltage

Turn the dial to the correct voltage setting (AC or DC). If your meter is manual-ranging, set it to the number just higher than the voltage you expect to measure (e.g., set to 20V for a 9V battery).

Step 3 of 4

Ensure power is ON

You can only measure voltage while electricity is actively flowing or available at the source.

Step 4 of 4

Place the probes

Place the red probe on the positive side of the component/source and the black probe on the negative side. If you get a negative reading (e.g., -9.00), your probes are simply reversed; it won't harm the meter.

Resistance (Ohms)

Resistance testing checks how much a component opposes the flow of electricity. It is crucial for testing resistors, potentiometers, and sensors.

Step 1 of 6

Power OFF circuit

Unplug the device or remove the battery. Never test resistance on a live circuit. External power will scramble the reading or blow the meter's internal fuse.

Step 2 of 6

Plug in the probes

Ensure probes are in the same ports used for voltage testing (Black to COM, Red to V/Ω/mA).

Step 3 of 6

Set the dial to Ohms (Ω)

Turn the dial to the Omega symbol (Ω). If it's manual-ranging, start at the lowest setting (like 200) and turn it up until you get a reading.

Step 4 of 6

Isolate the component

If the resistor is soldered into a complex board, measuring it in place might give a false reading. Desolder one leg if you need absolute accuracy.

Step 5 of 6

Place probes

Touch one probe to each end of the component. Resistors don't have polarity, so it doesn't matter which color goes where.

Step 6 of 6

Read the Display

If it reads 1 or OL (Open Loop), resistance is higher than your dial setting. Turn dial up. If it reads 0.00, there is zero resistance (short circuit).

Continuity

Continuity testing is a fast way to check if an electrical path is complete. It's essentially a very low resistance test that triggers a beeper. Perfect for finding broken wires or blown fuses.

Step 1 of 4

Power OFF circuit

Just like resistance testing, the circuit must be completely unpowered to prevent damage to the meter.

Step 2 of 4

Set dial to Continuity

Turn the dial to the icon that looks like a series of curved lines (sound wave/Wi-Fi symbol sideways) or a diode symbol.

Step 3 of 4

Test the meter first

Touch the metal tips of the red and black probes together. The meter should emit a continuous beep, and the screen should drop to near zero. This confirms it's working.

Step 4 of 4

Place probes on path

Touch one probe to one end of a wire or trace, and the other probe to the opposite end.

Beep: Path is continuous (good wire/fuse).
Silence / "OL": Path is broken.

How to Link Google Sheet

  1. Create a new Google Sheet and name it EPAS Student Assessment Data.
  2. In Google Sheets, go to Extensions > Apps Script.
  3. Delete existing code and paste the following script: 
    function doPost(e) {
  var sheet = SpreadsheetApp.getActiveSpreadsheet().getActiveSheet();
  if (sheet.getLastRow() === 0) {
    sheet.appendRow(["Timestamp", "Student Name", "Grade Level", "Section", "Lesson", "Score", "Total Possible"]);
  }
  var data = JSON.parse(e.postData.contents);
  sheet.appendRow([new Date(), data.name, data.grade, data.section, data.lesson, data.score, data.total]);
  return ContentService.createTextOutput(JSON.stringify({"result": "success"})).setMimeType(ContentService.MimeType.JSON);
}
  4. Click Deploy > New deployment.
  5. Select Web app. Set Who has access to Anyone.
  6. Click Deploy and copy the Web App URL.
  7. Assign the Google Apps Script Web App URL to the JavaScript submission logic or paste your Google Sheet link directly onto the link button to allow opening the spreadsheet directly!
